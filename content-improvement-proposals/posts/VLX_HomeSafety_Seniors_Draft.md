For many families, the hardest question is a simple one: can Mom or Dad still live safely at home? Aging in place — staying in one’s own home independently — is what most older adults want, and with the right adjustments it’s often possible. This home safety checklist for seniors helps you assess the house room by room and make that decision with confidence, focusing on the risk that sends more older adults to the hospital than any other: falls.

According to the CDC, about one in four adults 65 and older falls each year, and falls are the leading cause of injury in that group. Most happen at home, and most are preventable. [[Editor: confirm CDC figures are current before publishing.]]

## Can they still live safely at home? A quick decision framework

Before the room-by-room walk, step back and look at the whole picture. Use these questions to gauge whether home is still the right fit — and where support is needed:

- Mobility: can they move between rooms, and in and out of the home, safely on their own?
- Daily tasks: cooking, bathing, dressing, and medication — managed independently, or with help?
- Response: could they call for help after a fall or in an emergency?
- Cognition: any memory changes that affect stoves, locks, or medication?
- The home itself: stairs, lighting, and bathrooms — do they work for someone with reduced strength and balance?

A “no” doesn’t automatically mean a move — many gaps can be closed with home modifications, a caregiver, or a medical alert device. The checklist below shows where to start.

## Fall prevention, room by room

The bathroom is the single highest-risk room for older adults — wet surfaces, low seating, and tight spaces.

Start there.

### Bathroom (highest-risk zone)

- Grab bars mounted into studs or solid blocking (never just drywall anchors) beside the toilet and inside the tub/shower.
- Non-slip mats or adhesive strips in the tub and on the floor.
- A raised toilet seat and a shower chair if standing or lowering is difficult; a handheld showerhead.
- Water temperature capped (water heater at 120°F) to prevent scalds; good lighting.

### Lighting & pathways

- Bright, even lighting throughout; motion-sensor nightlights along the route from bed to bathroom.
- A clear, unobstructed path at night — no cords, clutter, or loose rugs.
- Light switches reachable from the bed and at both ends of hallways and stairs.

### Stairs, floors & furniture

- Secure handrails on both sides of every staircase; steps in good repair and well-lit.
- Remove or firmly anchor throw rugs (a top trip hazard); repair uneven flooring.
- Sturdy chairs with armrests to help with sitting and standing; nothing to lean on that could tip.

## Aging-in-place checklist — access & everyday tasks

- Frequently used items stored between waist and shoulder height to avoid reaching or bending.
- Lever-style door and faucet handles (easier than knobs for arthritic hands).
- A no-step or ramped entrance where possible; a bench near the door for putting on shoes.
- Easy-to-read labels on medications and a simple system (pill organizer or reminders).

## Medical alert devices

A wearable medical alert (a help button or fall-detection pendant) means help is one press away after a fall, even if a phone is out of reach. For someone living alone, it’s one of the highest-value additions on this list.

## Does Medicare cover a home safety assessment?

Coverage varies and changes, so confirm the specifics. In general, an occupational therapist (OT) may perform a home safety assessment, and OT services can be covered by Medicare when they’re medically necessary and ordered as part of care — but routine home modifications (grab bars, ramps) are typically not covered. For an older adult with memory changes, an OT assessment can also flag dementia-specific risks.

## Conclusion

Helping an older adult stay home safely comes down to two things: honestly assessing whether home still works, and closing the gaps you find. Walk through the framework and the rooms above, prioritize the bathroom and fall risks, add a medical alert if they live alone, and revisit it as needs change. Aging in place is most often a question of preparation — not luck.

**What are some home safety tips for seniors?**

Install grab bars mounted to studs in the bathroom, remove loose rugs, add motion-sensor nightlights and handrails on both sides of stairs, cap the water heater at 120°F, and consider a medical alert device — especially for someone living alone.

**Does Medicare cover home safety assessments?**

It depends. An occupational therapist’s assessment may be covered when medically necessary and ordered as part of care, but routine modifications like grab bars and ramps usually are not. Confirm current rules with Medicare or the provider.

**What should a home safety assessment for the elderly include?**

A room-by-room fall-hazard review (bathroom first), lighting and pathway checks, stair and handrail safety, medication and daily-task access, and an emergency-response plan such as a medical alert device.

**How do I know if an elderly parent can still live alone?**

Assess mobility, ability to manage daily tasks and medication, capacity to call for help after a fall, any memory changes, and whether the home’s layout is safe. Gaps can often be closed with modifications or support rather than a move.
