Buying or selling a mobile or manufactured home in California raises a question that trips up a lot of people: is a termite inspection actually required? The short answer is “it depends” — not always by law, but often by your lender, and almost always worth doing. This guide explains when it’s required, what the inspection covers, who pays, and what’s specific to mobile and manufactured homes.

## Is a termite inspection required in California?

California does not have a blanket law forcing a termite inspection on every sale. But in practice it’s often effectively required, for two reasons: your loan and your contract. Many purchase agreements call for a wood-destroying organism (WDO) report, and several loan programs require a clear termite report before they’ll fund — especially in higher-risk areas, which much of California is. So while no statute universally mandates it, the deal usually does. [[Editor: verify current statute/practice.]]

## Rules by loan type — Conventional, FHA, and VA

Whether a termite inspection is required most often comes down to how the buyer is financing the home:

- Conventional loans: usually no lender mandate, but the purchase contract or the appraiser may still call for a WDO report if there are visible signs.
- FHA loans: a termite/WDO inspection is commonly required when there’s evidence of infestation or the area is high-risk — and manufactured homes have their own HUD requirements on top.
- VA loans: a wood-destroying-pest inspection is generally required in most states, including California, and the report must typically show the home is clear or has been treated.

## What a WDO report covers — Section 1 vs Section 2

In California, the termite inspection produces a Wood Destroying Organisms (WDO) report from a licensed inspector, governed by the state’s Structural Pest Control Board (California Business & Professions Code §8516). The report splits findings into two sections:

- Section 1: active infestation or damage — live termites, fungus, or dry rot. These are the items usually required to be corrected for a clearance.
- Section 2: conditions that could lead to infestation (e.g., earth-to-wood contact, moisture) — recommended fixes, not always mandatory.

A “clear” report (or a termite clearance letter / certificate) is what lenders and buyers want to see before closing.

## The termite clearance letter — and how long it lasts

A termite clearance letter certifies that the home was inspected and is free of active infestation, or that required work was completed. Its validity is limited — generally in the range of 90 days to about six months depending on the lender and the report — so timing it near closing matters. Order it too early and it may expire before the deal funds. [[Editor: verify validity window with current lender/CA practice.]]

## What’s different about mobile & manufactured homes

This is where a mobile/manufactured home differs from a standard house — and where generic California termite content falls short. An inspector should pay special attention to:

- The underside of a raised structure — the belly wrap, floor joists, and framing under the home, where moisture and pests hide.
- Piers and supports where wood or the frame meets the ground or concrete.
- Skirting around the base, which can trap moisture and conceal activity.
- Additions and porches — site-built decks, steps, and rooms attached to the unit are common infestation points.

Because much of a manufactured home’s structure sits close to the ground, subterranean termites and moisture are the main concerns — and the access points differ from a home on a full foundation.

## Who pays for the termite inspection?

There’s no fixed rule — it’s negotiable and set in the purchase contract. Traditionally the seller often paid for the inspection and any Section 1 work in California, but that has shifted and varies by market and negotiation; today it frequently falls to the buyer. Sellers also carry disclosure obligations for known termite issues. Clarify who pays for the inspection and for any required repairs before you sign. [[Editor: confirm current norms; keep neutral.]]

## Detecting and preventing termites in a mobile home

Between transactions, a little vigilance goes a long way:

- Watch for mud tubes on piers and skirting, discarded wings, hollow-sounding wood, and small piles of frass (droppings).
- Keep soil, mulch, and firewood away from the base; fix leaks and improve drainage to cut moisture.
- Ventilate under the home and keep skirting from trapping water.
- Schedule periodic inspections — catching activity early is far cheaper than repairing structural damage.

## Conclusion

A termite inspection may not always be required by law in California, but for a mobile or manufactured home it’s usually required by your lender or your contract — and it’s cheap insurance against a hidden, expensive problem. Know your loan’s rules, get a WDO report from a licensed inspector, sort out who pays up front, and keep the clearance current through closing.

**Do mobile homes need to be treated for termites?**

Yes, when an inspection finds activity. Mobile and manufactured homes are vulnerable because much of the structure sits close to the ground; treatment is warranted if there are live termites or damage, and prevention (moisture control, clearances) helps avoid it.

## Is a termite inspection required in California?

Not by a universal law, but it’s often required by the loan program (commonly FHA and VA) or the purchase contract, and many California sales include a WDO report. In practice, most deals call for one.

**Does the buyer or seller pay for termite inspection in California?**

It’s negotiable and set in the contract. It has traditionally often fallen to the seller, but today it frequently falls to the buyer — agree on who pays for both the inspection and any required work before signing.

**How long is a termite inspection good for?**

A termite clearance is typically valid for about 90 days to six months, depending on the lender and the report, so it’s best timed close to closing.

**Can I do my own termite inspection?**

You can watch for signs (mud tubes, discarded wings, frass, hollow wood), but a WDO report accepted by lenders must come from a licensed inspector.
