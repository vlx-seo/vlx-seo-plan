A fire safety inspection is a systematic check of a building for the hazards that start fires and the systems that save lives when one breaks out. Whether it’s the home you live in or the workplace you’re responsible for, the goal is the same: find the risks before they find you.

This guide covers both. It explains what a fire safety inspection is and who performs it, gives you a room-by-room checklist for homes and a compliance-focused checklist for workplaces, and walks through the core systems every inspection examines — alarms, extinguishers, exits, electrical, and fire doors — with the NFPA standards behind them.

## What is a fire safety inspection, and who performs it?

A fire safety inspection is a review of a property against fire codes and safety standards to confirm that fire-prevention measures and life-safety systems are present, working, and maintained. It looks at two things at once: prevention (removing what could start or feed a fire) and protection (the alarms, extinguishers, and exits that limit harm if one does).

Who performs it depends on the setting. A homeowner can run a self-inspection with a checklist like the one below. Workplaces and commercial buildings are typically inspected by the local fire marshal or fire department, and often by a private fire-protection company for the alarm, sprinkler, and extinguisher servicing. In the U.S., inspections generally reference NFPA standards — NFPA 1 (Fire Code), NFPA 101 (Life Safety Code), NFPA 72 (alarms), and NFPA 10 (portable extinguishers) — alongside local code and, for workplaces, OSHA requirements.

## Home fire safety checklist

Work through your home room by room. Most home fire deaths involve a missing or non-working smoke alarm, so start there.

- Smoke alarms on every level, inside each bedroom, and outside each sleeping area. Test monthly; replace batteries yearly (or use 10-year sealed units); replace any alarm older than 10 years.
- Carbon monoxide alarms on each level and near sleeping areas, especially with gas appliances, a fireplace, or an attached garage.
- Kitchen: nothing flammable near the stovetop; a lid or fire blanket within reach; the range hood clean and vented.
- Heating: space heaters on a hard surface, 3 feet from anything combustible, and never left running unattended; furnace and chimney serviced annually.
- Electrical: no overloaded outlets or daisy-chained power strips; extension cords used only temporarily; no cords under rugs or through doorways.
- A home fire extinguisher (rated at least 2-A:10-B:C) on each level and in the kitchen — and everyone knows where it is and how to use it.
- Two ways out of every room, exits kept clear, and a practiced family escape plan with a meeting point outside.

## Workplace fire safety checklist (OSHA + commercial)

Workplaces carry legal obligations a home doesn’t. The checklist below reflects common OSHA and NFPA expectations; confirm the specifics with your local authority having jurisdiction (AHJ).

- Exits and egress: exit routes unobstructed and unlocked in the direction of travel; illuminated exit signs; emergency lighting that works on backup power.
- Fire extinguishers: the right type and rating for the hazard, mounted and unobstructed, inspected monthly and serviced annually (NFPA 10).
- Alarm and detection: a tested fire alarm system with audible/visible notification (NFPA 72); sprinklers, where present, with clearance below the heads.
- Electrical panels: a clear working space (OSHA requires roughly 36 inches) in front of panels; nothing stored against them.
- Flammable and combustible materials stored in approved containers/cabinets, away from ignition sources.
- Fire doors unblocked, self-closing, and never propped open.
- Documentation: current inspection records, an emergency action plan, and trained staff who know the evacuation routes.

## The core checks every inspection covers

Home or workplace, five systems do the heavy lifting. Here’s what a thorough inspection confirms for each.

### Detection & alarms

Smoke alarms and, where required, a monitored fire alarm system are present, powered (including battery backup), and tested. Test smoke and CO alarms monthly and replace units per the manufacturer — typically every 10 years. Commercial systems follow NFPA 72 for inspection and testing intervals.

### Fire extinguishers

Correct type for the hazard class, fully charged, unobstructed, and mounted at the proper height. A quick monthly visual check plus an annual professional service is the NFPA 10 baseline. (For how often extinguishers specifically must be checked, see our dedicated extinguisher guide — [[link to the extinguisher-frequency blog]].)

### Exits, exit signs, and clear egress paths

Every occupant needs at least two ways out. Exit routes stay clear and unlocked, exit signs are lit, and emergency lighting covers the path. This is the most-cited failure in workplace inspections — and the cheapest to fix: keep the path clear.

### Electrical hazards

Overloaded circuits, damaged cords, and permanent use of extension cords are leading ignition sources.

Panels need clear access, GFCI protection is used where required, and nothing combustible is stored against electrical equipment.

### Fire doors & compartmentation

In commercial buildings, fire-rated doors and walls slow a fire’s spread and protect exit routes. Fire doors must self-close and latch, stay unblocked, and never be propped open with a wedge.

## After the inspection

An inspection is only useful if the findings get acted on. Once you have the report:

1\. Read the report and understand each finding — what it is, where it is, and the code it references.

2\. Prioritize by risk. Life-safety items (blocked exits, dead alarms, missing extinguishers) come first; housekeeping items follow.

3\. Assign and schedule fixes, then re-check that each one is actually resolved.

4\. Keep the records. Dated reports, service tags, and photos are your proof of compliance for insurers and the AHJ.

## How often, and how much does it cost?

Frequency. Homes should be self-checked at least twice a year (a good habit: when the clocks change), with alarms tested monthly. Most workplaces are inspected annually by the fire authority, with extinguisher and alarm servicing on their own NFPA schedules — but your local code or occupancy type may require more.

Cost. A municipal workplace inspection is often low-cost or included in permitting; private inspections and system servicing vary by building size and equipment. Home self-inspections cost only your time plus the price of alarms and an extinguisher.

## FAQ

**What does a fire safety inspection consist of?**

It checks fire-prevention measures and life-safety systems: smoke and CO alarms, fire extinguishers, exits and exit signs, emergency lighting, electrical hazards, flammable-material storage, and (in commercial buildings) fire doors, alarms, and sprinklers — against fire code, NFPA standards, and, for workplaces, OSHA.

**What happens during a fire safety inspection?**

An inspector walks the property, tests or verifies each system, notes any violations with their location and the code involved, and issues a report. You then correct the findings, prioritizing life-safety items, and keep the records.

**How much does an annual fire inspection cost?**

It varies. Municipal workplace inspections are often low-cost or tied to permitting, while private inspections and equipment servicing depend on building size and systems. A home self-inspection costs only your time plus the alarms and extinguisher.

**Who can perform fire inspections?**

Homeowners can self-inspect with a checklist. Workplaces are inspected by the local fire marshal or fire department, often supported by a licensed fire-protection company for servicing alarms, sprinklers, and extinguishers.

**What are the NFPA 10 inspection requirements?**

NFPA 10 covers portable fire extinguishers: a monthly visual inspection (charge, access, condition) and an annual maintenance by a qualified professional, with periodic hydrostatic testing depending on the extinguisher type. See our dedicated extinguisher guide for the full schedule.
