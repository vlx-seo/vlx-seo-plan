Mold is easy to ignore until it isn’t — a musty smell, a dark patch behind the toilet, a spreading stain on a basement wall. A mold inspection checklist helps you find it early, in the places it likes to hide, before it damages the house or affects the people in it. This guide walks you through the whole home room by room, how to test what you find, and when to bring in a professional.

Use it as a homeowner doing a seasonal check, a landlord turning over a unit, or an inspector documenting a property. [[Editor: offer the downloadable checklist here.]]

## What is a mold inspection?

A mold inspection is a systematic look for visible mold, moisture, and the conditions that let mold grow. Mold needs three things: moisture, an organic surface to feed on (drywall, wood, dust), and time. Find and fix the moisture, and you solve the mold — which is why a good inspection is really a moisture inspection as much as a hunt for visible spots.

The process is straightforward: check the high-risk areas below, look for both visible mold and water signs, measure humidity where you can, and note anything that needs testing or professional remediation.

## Signs of mold in a house

Before the room-by-room walk, know what you’re looking for:

- A persistent musty or earthy smell, especially in closed rooms, basements, or closets.
- Visible spots — black, green, white, or orange — on walls, ceilings, grout, or wood.
- Water signs: stains, discoloration, peeling paint, bubbling, or warped surfaces.
- Condensation on windows or pipes, or high indoor humidity.
- Allergy-like symptoms that ease when you leave the house (per the CDC, mold can cause congestion, coughing, and irritation, especially for sensitive people). [[Editor: cite CDC.]]

## Room-by-room mold inspection checklist

Mold follows moisture, so focus on wet, warm, and poorly ventilated areas.

### Bathrooms

- Grout and caulk around the tub, shower, and sink; behind and under the toilet.
- The exhaust fan — present, working, and vented outside (not into the attic).
- Under-sink cabinet for leaks or water stains.

### Kitchen

- Under the sink and around the dishwasher and fridge water line.
- Behind and inside cabinets against exterior walls.
- Range hood venting and any window condensation.

### Basement & crawl space

- Walls and floor for stains, efflorescence, or seepage after rain.
- Around the water heater, sump pump, and any floor drains.
- Exposed framing and insulation for discoloration; musty odor.

### Attic

- Roof sheathing and rafters for dark staining (a sign of a roof leak or poor ventilation).
- Around plumbing vents and bathroom fan ducts.
- Insulation that’s damp, matted, or discolored.

### HVAC & laundry

- Around the AC drip pan, condensate line, and ductwork for moisture.
- The washing machine hose connections and behind the dryer (confirm it vents outside).
- Any room with a humidifier or standing water.

## How to test for mold

Once you spot a problem area, confirm it. You have two levels of tools:

- DIY: a home mold test kit (surface swab or air sample) gives a rough yes/no, and a moisture meter or hygrometer/humidistat tells you where moisture is high. Keep indoor humidity below about 50% to discourage growth (EPA guidance). [[Editor: cite EPA.]]
- Professional: a certified mold inspector uses infrared cameras, air sampling, and lab analysis to identify the type and extent — worth it for large areas, recurring mold, or a health concern.

## Black mold — when it’s serious

“Black mold” (often Stachybotrys) gets the most attention, but color alone doesn’t tell you the risk. What matters is the amount and whether people are reacting. The EPA notes that if the moldy area is larger than about 10 square feet, or you’re unsure, it’s best to hire a professional. [[Editor: verify EPA 10-sq-ft guidance is current.]]

## When to call a professional, and what it costs

Handle small spots (under ~10 sq ft) yourself with proper cleaning and moisture control. Call a professional when the area is large, mold keeps coming back, it’s in the HVAC system, there’s been flooding, or anyone in the home has respiratory issues. Inspection and remediation costs vary widely by size and location, so get more than one quote. [[Editor: keep cost as ranges; don’t state a single figure.]]

## Mold remediation checklist — after you find it

1\. Fix the moisture source first — the leak, condensation, or humidity. Cleaning without this just delays regrowth.

2\. Contain the area and protect yourself (gloves, N95, eye protection) per EPA cleanup guidance.

3\. Clean hard surfaces with detergent and water and dry completely; discard porous materials (drywall, carpet, ceiling tile) that are heavily molded.

4\. Dry the space fully and keep humidity down; re-check the area after a week.

5\. Document what you found, fixed, and removed — useful for insurance and future reference.

## Conclusion

Mold is a moisture problem before it’s a mold problem. Work through the room-by-room checklist, measure humidity, confirm what you find, and fix the water source — then keep a record. Catch it early and most mold is a weekend job; ignore it and it becomes a renovation.

**How do I prepare for a mold inspection?**

Clear access to likely problem areas (under sinks, the basement, the attic hatch, around the HVAC), note any past leaks or water damage, and list where you’ve smelled or seen mold. Fix obvious active leaks first if you can.

**What are the first signs of mold in a house?**

Usually a musty smell and small discolored spots in damp areas — bathrooms, under sinks, basements — along with water stains, peeling paint, or condensation.

**What time of year is worst for mold?**

Warm, humid months are highest-risk because mold thrives above ~50% humidity, but any season with leaks, flooding, or condensation can trigger growth. Control indoor humidity year-round.

**How much does a mold inspection cost?**

It varies by home size, location, and whether lab testing is involved. A basic visual inspection costs less than one with air sampling and lab analysis; get more than one quote for larger jobs.
