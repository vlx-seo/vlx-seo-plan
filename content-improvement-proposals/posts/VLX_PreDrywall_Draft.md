A pre-drywall inspection is your last clear look at how a house is actually built. Once the drywall goes up, the framing, wiring, plumbing, and ductwork disappear behind the walls — and any mistake hidden there becomes expensive to reach. This is the moment to catch it.

This pre-drywall inspection checklist walks through the 14 points a thorough walkthrough should cover, grouped by trade so you can move through the house room by room without missing anything. It’s written for the three people who care most about getting it right: home buyers verifying a new build, builders confirming their crews’ work, and inspectors documenting the structure before close-up.

## What is a pre-drywall inspection?

A pre-drywall inspection (sometimes called a frame inspection or pre-drywall walkthrough) is a review of a home’s structural, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems after the framing and rough-ins are complete but before insulation and drywall are installed. With the studs still exposed, an inspector can confirm that everything was built to plan and to code while corrections are still easy and cheap.

Timing matters. Schedule it after framing, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC rough-ins have passed the municipal inspections, and before the insulation crew arrives. That window is often just a few days, so coordinate with your builder early. Miss it, and you’re inspecting drywall instead of structure.

Why it’s worth doing: fixing a misplaced joist hanger or a notched load-bearing stud costs a carpenter an afternoon at this stage. After drywall, texture, and paint, the same fix means opening a finished wall — and eating the cost.

## The 14-point pre-drywall inspection checklist

Work through these in groups. Photograph every item as you go — those photos are your record if a dispute comes up after close-up.

### Framing & structure

1\. House frames. Walls plumb, level, and square; studs at correct spacing (typically 16" on center); no cracked, bowed, or over-notched members. Notches and holes in load-bearing studs must stay within code limits.

2\. Framed headers. Headers over every window and door opening are sized for the span and the load above them, and properly supported by jack (trimmer) studs.

3\. Beams. Girders and structural beams are the specified size and species, fully bearing at each end, with no splitting or crushing at the supports.

4\. Bottom plates. Sill/bottom plates are anchored to the subfloor or foundation, with pressure-treated plates used anywhere wood meets concrete or masonry.

5\. Rim boards. The rim/band joist is continuous, properly nailed, and tied into the floor system — it transfers load and braces the floor.

### Structural connectors

6\. Joist hangers. Correct hanger for each joist size, fully seated, and filled with the right nails or structural screws — not drywall or framing nails, and no empty nail holes.

7\. Hurricane clips. Where required by wind code, metal ties connect the roof/trusses to the top plate and the walls to the framing below, with every nail hole filled.

8\. Metal beams / connectors. Steel beams, columns, and post caps/bases are the specified hardware, bolted as detailed, and not substituted for a lighter part.

### Building envelope

9\. Windows & exterior doors. Set plumb, level, and square in the rough opening; flashed and sealed against water; and operating freely before the walls close.

10\. Shims used for windows and doors. Shims are placed at anchor points to keep frames straight without bowing the jambs — over-shimming or gaps behind the frame cause operating and air-leak problems later.

11\. Siding or masonry veneer. Exterior cladding and its weather-resistive barrier are installed with proper laps and flashing so water drains out, not into the wall.

### Mechanical, electrical & plumbing (rough-in)

12\. Subfloor. Sheathing is the correct thickness, glued and fastened on schedule, with no squeaks, soft spots, or water damage before finishes go down.

13\. HVAC, electrical & plumbing systems. Ducts are sealed and supported; wiring is secured and boxed with no exposed splices; supply and drain lines are pressure/leak-tested and properly sloped. Confirm each trade’s municipal rough-in inspection has passed.

14\. Framing & insulation. Fire blocking and firestopping are in place at required cavities and penetrations, metal nail plates protect any pipe or wire within 1¼" of a stud edge, and framing is ready for the insulation crew.

## Who attends, and what to expect after

On a new build, the pre-drywall walkthrough usually brings together the buyer, the builder or site superintendent, and often an independent third-party inspector hired by the buyer. Bringing your own inspector matters: the builder’s crews and the municipal inspector are checking their own work, while a third party works only for you.

Walk the whole house, not just the rooms you care about. Take dated photos of anything questionable and note its location so it can be found again once the walls are closed. If you spot an issue, raise it on the spot and get the builder’s commitment to fix it in writing.

After the walkthrough you should come away with:

- A written list of any items to correct, each tied to a location and a photo.
- The builder’s agreement on what gets fixed and by when — before insulation and drywall begin.
- A re-check of the corrected items (or photo confirmation) so nothing gets buried unresolved.

Once you sign off, insulation goes in and drywall follows. That’s why the pre-drywall stage is effectively one-way: this checklist is your leverage while the structure is still open.

## Is a pre-drywall inspection worth it? Cost and timing

For most buyers, yes. A pre-drywall inspection is a small, one-time cost against the price of a home — and it buys you a look at the parts of the house you can never see again once it’s finished. The findings are also easiest and cheapest to fix at this exact stage.

What it typically costs. A third-party pre-drywall inspection generally runs a few hundred dollars, depending on the size of the home and your market. Many buyers pair it with a final walkthrough inspection near closing so the same inspector sees the house open and complete.

How long it takes. Expect roughly two to four hours on site for an average single-family home — longer for larger or more complex builds — plus a day or so for the written report and photos. Because the pre-drywall window is short, book your inspector as soon as the rough-ins pass so you’re not the reason drywall gets delayed.

## FAQ

**What is included in a pre-drywall inspection?**

It covers the home’s structure and rough-ins while the studs are still exposed: framing (walls, headers, beams, plates, rim boards), structural connectors (joist hangers, hurricane clips, metal hardware), the building envelope (windows, doors, flashing, siding), and the HVAC, electrical, and plumbing rough-ins — plus fire blocking, nail plates, and the subfloor.

**How long does a pre-drywall inspection take?**

About two to four hours on site for a typical single-family home, plus roughly a day for the written report and photos. Larger or more complex homes take longer.

**Is a pre-drywall inspection worth it?**

For most buyers, yes. It’s a small, one-time cost that lets you verify the parts of the house you’ll never see again after drywall — and any problems found are far cheaper to fix at this stage.

**What needs to be done before drywall?**

Framing and all rough-ins (electrical, plumbing, HVAC) should be complete and have passed their municipal inspections, with fire blocking and nail plates installed. The inspection happens in the short window after those approvals and before insulation and drywall go in.
